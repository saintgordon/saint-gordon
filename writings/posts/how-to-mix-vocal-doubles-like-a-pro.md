Great vocal doubles wrap around a lead like a wide frame without ever sounding messy or distracting. Yet copying your lead track onto two panned channels doesn’t add width at all: identical audio panned equally left and right collapses straight back into louder mono, and sliding a duplicate by a few milliseconds triggers hollow **comb filtering** the moment anyone listens on a phone speaker.

> Never duplicate a lead statically. Record separate takes, trim the consonants, carve room with EQ, and tuck them underneath.

## 1. Record Real Doubles & Lock Phrase Endings

Two genuine takes naturally carry subtle micro differences in pitch, vibrato, and timing that fool the ear into wide stereo depth without collapsing in mono. *(If you are stuck with one single track, reach for subtle micro pitch shifting of 5 to 10 cents or wide slapback delay, and check your mono sum constantly.)*

Decide how tight the track needs to feel, then edit **word endings and cutoffs** first. If the lead snaps a syllable shut cleanly while a double lingers behind, the whole chorus feels sloppy.

## 2. Tame Consonants & De Ess Harder Than the Lead

Sharp `"T"`, `"P"`, `"K"`, and `"S"` bursts on wide left and right tracks tear at listeners’ ears whenever two takes miss by a fraction of a second. Pull down harsh consonant spikes with clip gain, then run a dedicated **de esser** on your doubles *before* compression so only the centred lead vocal carries primary sibilance.

## 3. Sculpt Doubles Around the Lead

Never paste your lead vocal EQ straight onto your doubles:

* **High Pass Higher (`150 to 300 Hz`):** Strip low end mud and chest weight out of the sides (`80 to 100 Hz` belongs strictly to the centre lead).
* **Gentle Top Roll Off (`10 to 14 kHz`) & Mid Dip:** Shave off extreme top air and try a `1 to 3 dB` dip in the `1 to 3 kHz` vocal presence zone so doubles wrap behind the lead instead of fighting it. Light tape or tube saturation also thickens doubles smoothly.

## 4. Compress, Pan Symmetrically & Group by Rhythm

Compress doubles slightly harder and steadier (`LA2A` or slower attack with fast release) so stereo sides never poke unpredictably over the lead. Route unison doubles that sing the **exact same rhythm** as the lead into a shared vocal bus or shared parallel compressor to glue them together, while keeping rhythmically different backing harmonies on their own separate compressor so one part never pumps the other.

Pan left and right takes equal and opposite (`50%` left/right for tight core thickness, `100%` hard left/right for wide choruses, or both pairs stacked), then tuck the doubles roughly **`6 to 12 dB` quieter** than the lead. Check your finished mix in mono inside [Pre Flight](https://saintgordon.vercel.app/tools#tool-pre-flight) to make sure the vocal never hollows out.
